An Awful Lot Lining-up Here and it May Mean Something
A speculative note, in three tiers
Ian Beardsley
November 3, 2026
There is a way of writing about coincidence that ruins the coincidence. You list everything that
lines up, you let the list do the persuading, and the reader either buys it wholesale or dismisses it
wholesale. Neither response is useful.
This note tries something else. It puts the observations in three tiers. The first tier is what the
papers in this collection have established and would defend in front of a referee. The second tier
is suggestive but not established — real observations that point somewhere, without yet pointing
hard enough to be called evidence. The third tier is speculation, stated as speculation, and offered
because the question is worth asking even when the answer is not yet available.
The question is this: a surprising number of things about the Earth–Moon system converge on
the present epoch. Is that a coincidence, a selection effect, or something structural? I do not
know. But the convergence is real, and it is worth laying out carefully enough that someone else
can judge it.
Tier 1: The framework, and what it establishes
The material in this tier is drawn from four papers, and it is what the collection would defend.
Because the convergence argument depends on the mathematics, the framework is set out below
in the order the papers develop it: the law, its application to the Earth, its application to the
galaxy, and what follows from all three about the present epoch.

The epoch-dependence. The Earth relation depends on the Moon's orbital radius. At formation it
would have returned ~0.61 s; in the far future it will return ~1.40 s. The present epoch is when it
returns ~0.98 s.
The galactic nodes. The same procedure applied at the galactic scale returns order-one seconds at
the asteroid belt and (more approximately) at Saturn's orbit.
The perfect eclipse is behind us. The exact match of lunar and solar angular sizes at mean
distance was crossed in the opposite direction in the distant past. Estimates range from a few
million to several hundred million years ago, with the commonly quoted figure around 150–300
Myr. Total eclipses remain possible today because the lunar orbit is elliptical and the Moon is
closer at perigee; they will cease in a few hundred million years.
The resonance is ahead of us. On standard tidal models, exact closure is reached in roughly 175–
250 million years. The present epoch sits between two crossings: the eclipse behind, the
resonance ahead.
What is not established is anything about why these near-matches hold. The framework reports
them. It does not derive them. Whether the particle-scale recurrence, the Earth near-match, and
the galactic nodes reflect a common underlying structure, or are a series of related numerical
coincidences, or are a selection effect of the epoch at which observers can exist, is the open
question. The papers are careful about this and so is this note.
Tier 2: Suggestive, not established
The material in this tier is real observation that points somewhere, without yet pointing hard
enough to count as evidence. It is the material I find most interesting, and the material most
likely to change if better data arrive.
2.1 The Mars control case
Mars is the solar system's natural control for the large-moon hypothesis. It has no large moon —
Phobos and Deimos together are less than a ten-millionth of Mars's mass — and the
consequences are exactly what the hypothesis predicts.

The obliquity contrast is the cleanest part of this. Earth's tilt has been held steady for millions of
years. Mars's tilt has swung through tens of degrees on timescales of hundreds of thousands to
millions of years, because there is no large satellite to damp the solar and Jovian torques. That is
the large-moon hypothesis making a prediction and the solar system providing the
counterexample.
The day-length contrast is nearly as clean, and it is the one that connects to the resonance. Earth's
day has been braked by lunar tides — the Moon raises a bulge, the bulge leads the Moon because
Earth rotates faster than the Moon orbits, and the resulting torque transfers angular momentum
from Earth's spin to the Moon's orbit. The Moon recedes; the day lengthens. Mars has no such
engine. Its moons are too small to raise significant tides, and Phobos orbits faster than Mars
rotates, which would speed the planet up rather than slow it down. The InSight lander recently
measured a tiny acceleration of Mars's rotation — a shortening of the day by about
milliseconds per year — which is the opposite sign to Earth's tidal braking and roughly a
thousand times larger than the Sun's tidal deceleration would predict.
Earth has a 24-hour day because it has a large moon. Mars has roughly a 24-hour day in spite of
having no large moon, by coincidence of formation. The two planets arrived at similar day
lengths by entirely different routes.
The caveats are real. Mars is smaller, drier, farther from the Sun, and has no oceans. This is a
two-point comparison, not a controlled experiment. But it is the only such comparison available
in the solar system, and the direction of the difference is what the hypothesis predicts.

The third line matters more than it first appears. The equation says that the resonance value is a
function of the Moon's orbital kinetic energy and the planet's rotation rate. Change the day to
twelve hours and the output halves; change it to forty-eight and it doubles. The "24-hour day"
and the "1-second resonance" are therefore not two independent coincidences that happen to
coincide. The equation maps one onto the other at this epoch.
This has a consequence worth stating plainly. Both variables are evolving in the same direction.
The Moon is receding, which increases . The Earth's day is lengthening, which also increases .
The two effects compound. The resonance will be crossed sooner than the Moon's recession
alone would suggest, because the planet's spin is slowing at the same time.
The present epoch is the moment when two independently evolving quantities happen to produce
a product near unity. That is a different kind of coincidence from "the Moon is at the right
distance." It is "the Moon is at the right distance and the Earth is spinning at the right rate," and
both are transient.
2.3 The galactic year
The time to exact closure is 175–250 Myr on the tidal models. The Sun's orbital period around
the galactic center — the "galactic year" — is approximately 225–250 Myr. The central estimates
differ by roughly 20%, and the model uncertainty on the closure time is of the same order, so the
two ranges overlap without the agreement being sharp. The honest statement is that the closure
time brackets the galactic year.
I do not know what to make of this. There is no mechanism connecting terrestrial tidal
dissipation to the Sun's orbit through the galactic potential. And the galactic year is essentially
the only astronomical timescale in this range: the Sun's vertical oscillation across the galactic
plane is 60–90 Myr, spiral arm passage is poorly defined and on the order of 100 Myr, and the
Milankovitch cycles are all below 1 Myr. Finding one candidate and getting a 20% match is what
you would expect from a single draw.
It is worth recording. It is not worth building on. The relevant discipline is the one the collection
applies in its §9: a pattern claim is only as strong as the size of the space it was selected from,
and this space has size one.
One related piece of literature is worth noting, because it is often confused with this one. Rohde
and Muller (2005) reported a ~62 Myr cycle in marine genus diversity, which they attributed to
the Sun's vertical oscillation across the galactic plane. That claim is itself contested, but it is the
well-established version of "a galactic timescale shows up in the terrestrial record." It is not the
galactic year, and it does not support the coincidence described above. It is a different claim with
a different mechanism, and it should not be recruited into this one.

3.1 Lithopanspermia
The idea that life on Earth may have originated on Mars is not fringe — it has a name, a
literature, and a plausible mechanism. Mars is smaller, so it cooled and solidified and developed
liquid water earlier. Earth was reset around 4.5 billion years ago by the impact that formed the
Moon, melting its surface; Mars avoided that particular catastrophe. And Martian rocks blasted
into space by impacts can carry microbial life in their interiors, surviving ejection, transit, and
atmospheric entry.
The astrobiological case for Mars as the first habitable world in the solar system is stronger now
than it was a decade ago. Perseverance has found organic molecules and intriguing mineral
textures in Jezero Crater. Curiosity found long-chain carbon molecules in mudstone roughly 3.7
billion years old. None of this is proof of life, and none of it establishes that life came to Earth
from Mars rather than the other way around, or that either planet originated it.
I mention it because it is a second respect in which Earth may not be the first — the first in the
sequence of habitable worlds, and the beneficiary of whatever Mars managed to do before Earth
was ready. Whether that matters for the resonance argument is not clear. But if the resonance is a
condition for habitability, it is worth asking which planet satisfied it first.
3.2 The entrainment thought experiment
Here is the most speculative item in this note, and I want to be clear that I do not believe it. I
offer it because it is the kind of question that follows naturally from the rest, and because saying
why it fails is more useful than not mentioning it.
The Martian sol is 24 hours, 39 minutes, 35 seconds — about 2.75% longer than Earth's day. The
Martian obliquity is currently 25.2°, close to Earth's 23.4°. The kinetic-energy equation that
maps the day length into the resonance takes as an input, and the Martian and terrestrial
values are close enough that the equation would return a similar number for Mars.
Could Martian life, if it existed, have been entrained to a rhythm comparable to Earth's?
Almost certainly not, for three reasons. There is no evidence that Martian life ever existed. If it
did, it would have been entrained to the Martian sol, not Earth's — a 2.75% difference in period
is large enough that circadian systems distinguish them; biological oscillators are sensitive to
differences far smaller. And the 24-hour figure for Earth is a present-epoch value: Earth's day
was much shorter in the past, so any early Martian life would not have matched early Earth life
either.
The idea fails. It is worth stating because it illustrates a general trap: near-matches between
planetary parameters are easy to find and easy to over-read. The Martian sol and the Earth day

3.3 What "may mean something" could mean
The question the title asks is whether the convergence of these observations means something.
There are three possible answers, and they are the same three the collection applies to the one-
second resonance itself.
Coincidence. The Moon is anomalously massive because of a giant impact whose parameters
happened to be extreme. The present epoch is one of many epochs in Earth's history, and it
happens to be the one at which two independent timescales cross near unity. The eclipse is a
transient geometric accident. The galactic year proximity is a coincidence of a coincidence.
Nothing needs explaining; it is just where we are.
Selection. Observers must exist at an epoch when the conditions for their existence are met. If a
large moon and a stable obliquity are among those conditions, then the epoch at which the
resonance is legible is not a random sample of Earth's history — it is the sample from which
observers can be drawn. The convergence is then not a coincidence but a selection effect, and it
tells us about the conditions for observers rather than about the structure of the universe.
Structure. There is a deeper reason why the Moon's mass ratio, the Earth's rotation rate, and the
resonance condition are correlated. Perhaps the same dynamics that produce large moons also
select for the mass ratios at which the resonance is satisfiable. Perhaps the timescale correlations
reflect something about how bound systems organize across scales. This is the strongest claim
and the one with the least support. The collection does not make it.
The honest position is that the evidence so far is consistent with all three, and that distinguishing
them requires data the solar system cannot provide. Exomoons around other stars would help. A
population of satellite systems with measured mass ratios and obliquities would let us ask
whether the Earth–Moon configuration is typical or rare, and whether the resonance condition is
satisfied across the population or only here.
Until then, what can be said is this. There is more lining up here than one would expect from a
random draw, and less than would justify a claim. The right response to that situation is not to
decide which it is, but to record what lines up, say which parts are load-bearing and which are
decoration, and specify what would change the picture.
That is what this note has tried to do.
